CRO-10 Still Head

Congener Recovery Optimized Design for a 10-Gallon Custom Pot Still

Design goal: Maximize recovery of light fruity esters (ethyl acetate +39%, isoamyl acetate +32%) while chemically eliminating sulfur compounds (DMS, H2S) through a copper-packed reactive column. The CRO-10 extends the usable hearts cut from ~40% to ~55% of total condensate by removing DMS via CuO-mediated oxidation. Honest trade-off: the packed column that removes DMS also creates passive reflux that strips heavy body congeners (ethyl octanoate ~0%, furfural −6% vs pot still). Fusel recovery increases +27%. See §6.3 for a simpler alternative (Cu mesh in pot) that preserves heavy congeners.
Safety & Legal — READ BEFORE BUILDING
  • Overpressure: A pressure relief valve (PRV) on the pot lid is mandatory. A sealed still without relief is a pipe bomb if the condenser blocks or a joint fails. Test PRV before every run.
  • Fire/explosion: Ethanol vapor is heavier than air (density 1.6× air) and highly flammable. The lower explosive limit (LEL) is 3.3% by volume. Vapors pool at floor level. Operate outdoors or with forced ventilation. Keep a 2A:10B:C fire extinguisher within arm’s reach. Never leave an operating still unattended.
  • No open flames near the vapor path: If using propane for the pot, the burner must be shielded from the condenser outlet. A propane leak near ethanol vapor is a catastrophic ignition risk. Electric heating eliminates this hazard and is strongly recommended.
  • Carbon monoxide (CO): Propane burners produce CO. In enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces, CO buildup can be lethal without warning. Use a CO detector. Electric heat is inherently safer.
  • Methanol: All fermented washes contain trace methanol. Foreshots (first 50–100 mL) concentrate methanol and must be discarded, never consumed. Methanol is toxic: 10 mL can cause blindness, 30 mL can be fatal. Label foreshot containers clearly.
  • Burns and scalds: All copper and stainless surfaces above the pot reach 80–100°C during operation. Wear heat-resistant gloves when adjusting TC clamps or the needle valve. Hot condensate can cause severe scalds.
  • Electrical safety (if using electric heat): Use a GFCI-protected circuit. Ensure the heating element is rated for wet/outdoor use. Never operate electrical equipment near standing water or spilled condensate.
  • Emergency procedure: If a leak develops during a run: (1) shut off heat, (2) do NOT attempt to disconnect the still while hot, (3) ventilate the area, (4) let the system cool before disassembly. If fire occurs: use the extinguisher, then evacuate.
  • Legal: In the United States, a Federal Distilled Spirits Permit (DSP) is required under 27 CFR §19. Many other countries also require permits. Check local regulations before construction or operation.

1. Design Philosophy

1.1 The Pot-vs-Column Tradeoff

Every still designer faces the same fundamental tension:

  • Pot stills produce flavorful but “dirty” spirit. Minimal separation means esters, aldehydes, and higher alcohols all carry into the distillate—along with dimethyl sulfide (DMS, cooked-corn off-flavor). An all-copper pot provides some DMS removal (~55%) through CuO-catalyzed oxidation, but the hearts DMS can still exceed the ~25 ppb sensory threshold.
  • Column stills produce clean but characterless spirit. With 6+ theoretical plates and high reflux, columns rectify away virtually all congeners. A 6-plate column recovers only 0.5% of ethyl hexanoate (apple) vs 23% for a pot still—flavor is sacrificed for purity. Even a packed column at R=0 still strips heavy congeners via passive reflux from heat loss through the column wall.

1.2 The CRO Solution: Decouple Separation from Sulfur Removal

The CRO-10 resolves this tradeoff by recognizing that sulfur removal and congener separation are different physical processes:

  • Sulfur removal is chemical: CuO on the packing surface mediates the oxidation of DMS (dimethyl sulfide, (CH3)2S) to DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide, (CH3)2SO). DMSO is water-soluble and non-volatile, so it drains back down the column in the reflux liquid. Important nuance: this reaction is stoichiometric in CuO within a single run—the copper oxide is consumed as it oxidizes DMS. Between runs, air exposure re-oxidizes the copper surface (Cu/Cu2O → CuO), regenerating the reactive sites. The process requires copper surface area, not theoretical plates. Separately, H2S reacts irreversibly with copper: Cu + H2S → CuS + H2↑ (this consumes copper permanently and is not regenerated by air).
  • Congener separation is physical: Ester recovery depends on reflux ratio, plate count, and cut decisions. A variable dephlegmator (water-cooled coil inside the column) allows real-time tuning: high reflux during foreshots to strip volatiles, zero reflux during hearts to maximize ester passage.

By packing the column with copper Raschig rings, we get both functions in a single unit: chemical DMS removal at all reflux settings, and physical separation only when the dephlegmator coil is actively cooled.

2. Physics & Simulation Results

2.1 Congener Recovery Comparison

The Rayleigh batch distillation model predicts the fraction of each congener that appears in the hearts cut. For component i with relative volatility αrel = Ki/Kethanol and effective plates Neff:

Recoveryi = (1 − f1)Ei − (1 − f2)Ei   where  Ei = αrelNeff

The CRO-10 extends the hearts window (10–65% vs the pot still’s typical 15–55%). With copper removing DMS, the wider cut captures more light esters. However, the packed column creates ~0.3 additional effective stages from passive reflux (heat loss through uninsulated Cu wall), which selectively condenses heavy congeners on ring surfaces. Compounds with bp >> 100°C (like 2-phenylethanol at 219°C or ethyl octanoate at 207°C) are present in vapor only at trace levels via Raoult’s law partitioning; each ring surface provides a condensation opportunity that preferentially strips them. The net result: CRO gains +30–40% for light esters (α>0.7) but is neutral or harmful for heavy body compounds (α<0.3). Fusel recovery increases ~27%. Insulating the column would reduce passive reflux and improve heavy congener passage.

2.3 Copper DMS Removal: PFR Model

DMS removal in the packed copper column follows a plug-flow reactor (PFR) model for CuO-mediated oxidation:

removal = 1 − exp(−keff · as · L / vs)

where keff = 0.16 cm/s is a lumped effective rate constant (encompassing surface reaction rate, CuO utilization fraction, and flow channeling; calibrated from pot still Cu removal data, ~55%, reported in Piggott 2003), as ≈ 7.0 cm²/cm³ (specific surface area for 3/8″ Raschig rings, ≈700 m²/m³, Perry’s §14), L = packed bed length (cm), and vs = superficial vapor velocity (cm/s).

Model uncertainty: keff is calibrated from a single empirical data point (pot still DMS removal) and extrapolated to a packed column with very different geometry. Uncertainty is large: keff ∈ [0.08, 0.24] cm/s (±50%). All DMS removal numbers below should be read as central estimates with a range of roughly ±15 percentage points.

At 3 kW heat input (vs ≈ 20 cm/s through the 99 mm ID column), the 12″ packed bed achieves ~82% DMS removal (range: 60–93%). Lowering heat to 2 kW (vs ≈ 13 cm/s) increases removal to ~93% (range: 73–98%). Practical guidance: run slower for cleaner spirit.

4. Build Instructions

4.1 Tools Required

  • Propane torch (MAPP gas preferred for 4″ joints—standard propane may struggle with the thermal mass)
  • Tube cutter for 2″ and 4″ copper pipe
  • Sandpaper/emery cloth (120 grit) for joint prep
  • Step drill or hole saw (4″) for pot lid modification
  • Drill + 1/4″ bit for water port holes
  • Tri-clamp ferrules (buy pre-made—much easier than fabricating)
  • Ruler, protractor, marker
  • Tube bender for 1/4″ Cu coil (or hand-form around a 3.5″ cylinder)

4.2 Step 1: Pot Modification

  1. Mark center of pot lid. Drill pilot hole, then cut 4″ hole with step drill or hole saw.
  2. Insert 4″ TC bulkhead ferrule from underside. Secure with stainless bolts, washers, and food-grade silicone gasket. Do not attempt to TIG weld unless you have SS welding equipment and experience—bolt-on is the practical solution.
  3. Install PRV: drill a 1.5″ hole near the rim of the lid. Mount 1.5″ TC ferrule and install the 2 PSI pressure relief valve. This is not optional—a sealed still without relief is a pipe bomb if the condenser blocks.
  4. Test seal: fill inverted lid with water, check for leaks at all ferrule joints.

4.3 Step 2: Column Assembly

  1. Cut pipe: 4″ Cu DWV pipe → two sections: 12″ (vapor rise) and 18″ (packed section + dephlegmator zone).
  2. Solder TC ferrules to both ends of the vapor rise section. Clean pipe ends with emery cloth, apply flux, heat evenly with MAPP torch, feed lead-free silver solder. Let cool naturally—do not quench.
  3. Dephlegmator coil: Wind 10’ of 1/4″ Cu refrigeration tubing into a coil of ~3.5″ diameter (5 wraps). This will sit inside the top 6″ of the 18″ column section, above the packing. Drill two 1/4″ holes through the column wall for water in/out. Thread coil ends through the holes and solder them in place.
  4. Solder TC ferrule to the bottom of the 18″ section. Solder the 4″→2″ reducer to the top.
  5. Insert mesh screen at bottom of packed section (held by TC ferrule lip). Pour in 3/8″ Cu Raschig rings to 12″ depth (the coil sits above). Place second mesh screen on top of rings below the coil. Shake gently to settle.
  6. Insulate the column: Wrap the vapor rise and packed sections in 1″ ceramic fiber or fiberglass pipe insulation. This reduces passive reflux from heat loss, improving heavy congener passage during hearts (R=0). Leave the dephlegmator section uninsulated so it can radiate when engaged. This single step significantly changes the flavor profile of the spirit.
  7. Verify: Assemble vapor rise + packed/dephleg section with TC clamps. Check alignment. Blow through to verify airflow.

4.4 Step 3: Lyne Arm

  1. Cut 2″ Cu pipe to 18″.
  2. Solder 2″ TC ferrule to one end (connects to reducer atop column).
  3. The other end connects to the condenser. Either solder directly or use another TC fitting.
  4. Set angle: When mounted, the lyne arm slopes 10° downward from column to condenser. Mark this angle on your mounting structure. Use a support bracket or clamp to a shelf/wall.

4.5 Step 4: Liebig Condenser

  1. Inner tube: Cut 2″ Cu pipe to 24″.
  2. Jacket: Cut 3″ Cu pipe to 22″. Drill 1/4″ holes for water inlet (bottom end) and outlet (top end). Solder 1/4″ tube stubs.
  3. End caps: Cut two 3″ Cu discs. Drill 2″ center holes. Solder inner tube through both caps, then solder caps to jacket body. Critical: all joints must be watertight.
  4. Outlet reducer: Solder 2″ → 1/2″ reducer to the exit end.
  5. Pressure test the water jacket: cap outlet, fill jacket, pressurize to 20 PSI. Hold 5 minutes. No leaks = pass.

4.6 Step 5: Final Assembly

  1. Clamp column base (vapor rise) to pot lid TC ferrule with silicone gasket.
  2. Stack packed/dephleg section on top via TC clamp.
  3. Attach lyne arm to the 4″→2″ reducer at column top. Support independently—do not let the lyne arm’s weight stress the solder joints.
  4. Attach condenser to lyne arm end. Support independently.
  5. Connect water supply: source → needle valve → dephlegmator coil inlet → dephlegmator coil outlet → condenser inlet (bottom) → condenser outlet (top) → drain.
  6. Place collection vessel under condenser outlet with parrot + hydrometer.
  7. Verify PRV function: Before first run, gently pressurize pot to confirm the PRV opens at ~2 PSI.

4.7 Step 6: Conditioning Run

First run: Charge with a sacrificial wash (cheap sugar wash or 50:50 water:white vinegar). Run a full distillation to:
  • Flush solder flux residues from all copper joints
  • Season copper surfaces (initial CuO formation improves sulfur reactivity)
  • Verify all joints are sealed under operating temperature
  • Verify PRV does not activate under normal operation (it should not if condenser water is flowing)
  • Practice cut decisions and dephlegmator control
Discard all distillate from the conditioning run.

5. Operating Guide

5.1 Charge and Startup

  1. Charge pot with 8–9 gallons of low wines (~25% ABV) or wash (~10% ABV).
  2. Assemble column, ensure all TC clamps are tight. Verify PRV is in place.
  3. Start condenser water flow first (cold water through condenser jacket).
  4. Apply heat at 2–3 kW. Bring pot to a slow boil over 30–45 minutes. Do not exceed 3.5 kW—at 4 kW the column operates at 70–100% of flooding capacity, risking unstable operation. Stay at 3 kW or below for the best DMS removal and operating margin.
  5. When vapor reaches the thermocouple (temperature rises above ambient), you are ready to begin the protocol.

The single most important adjustment: Close the dephlegmator needle valve during hearts. With R≈0, the copper column acts purely as a chemical DMS scrubber—zero separation, maximum flavor passage. This is what makes the CRO different from a standard reflux column.

5.3 Cut Decision Guides

  1. Temperature: The thermocouple at the column top tracks vapor composition. Rising temperature = heavier compounds arriving.
  2. Taste/smell: Use a smell strip or clean spoon. Foreshots smell like nail polish. Hearts smell clean and grainy. Tails smell waxy/oily.
  3. Hydrometer (in parrot): Hearts typically exit at 65–72% ABV. When ABV drops below 60%, you are entering late hearts/tails.

5.4 Cleaning and Maintenance

  • After every run: Rinse column sections with hot water. Flush water through dephlegmator coil and condenser jacket. Allow copper to air dry.
  • Every 5 runs: Remove Raschig rings. Soak in citric acid solution (2 tbsp per gallon water) for 30 min. Rinse, dry, repack.
  • Inspect TC gaskets every 10 runs. Replace if cracked or compressed.
  • Copper patina: Green/brown patina is normal and beneficial. CuO and Cu2O are more reactive with sulfur compounds than bare Cu. Do not polish interior surfaces.
  • PRV: Test annually by gently pressurizing pot. Replace if it fails to open at rated pressure.

6. Expected Performance

6.1 Congener Targets (Rayleigh Model, corrected for passive reflux)

Critical nuance — passive reflux in packed columns: Even with the dephlegmator off (R=0), the 12″ of copper packing creates passive reflux: heat loss through the uninsulated column wall (~140 W at ΔT=58°C) condenses vapor on ring surfaces. Heavy congeners (low α, high boiling point) are preferentially condensed—a 2-phenylethanol molecule (bp 219°C) will condense on a ring at 80°C even though trace amounts exist in the vapor via Raoult’s law partitioning.

This passive reflux adds ~0.3 effective theoretical stages to the CRO during “R=0” hearts collection (Neff ≈ 1.3 vs 1.0 for a simple pot still). Light esters (α > 0.7) still benefit from the wider cut. Heavy esters and body compounds (α < 0.3) see minimal benefit or slight loss compared to a pot still. Insulating the column would reduce this effect.

6.3 Alternative: Copper Mesh in Pot (No Column)

For flavor-forward spirits: If preserving heavy body congeners (ethyl octanoate, furfural, 2-phenylethanol) is the priority, consider an alternative approach: copper mesh packed in the pot headspace instead of a packed column.

Copper mesh (1–2 reams of Cu scrubber pads) placed above the liquid surface in the pot headspace provides CuO surface area for DMS oxidation. Vapor contacts the mesh en route to the lyne arm. Key advantages:

  • No passive reflux penalty: The mesh sits in the hot headspace at full boiling temperature, not in a tall column with heat loss. Heavy congeners pass through with minimal selective condensation.
  • DMS removal: Estimated 30–50% DMS removal from the mesh alone (in addition to 30–40% from the copper still body). Combined: ~50–70% total DMS removal. Not as high as the CRO column (~82%), but enough to keep DMS below or near the 25 ppb threshold for many washes.
  • Simplicity and cost: $10–20 in copper mesh vs $500–800 for the full CRO column. No tri-clamp fittings, no dephlegmator plumbing.
  • Full heavy congener preservation: 2-phenylethanol, ethyl octanoate, and other body compounds pass through the headspace mesh without the stripping that occurs in a packed column.

When to choose the CRO column over mesh:

  • Your wash has high DMS (>80 ppb) and you need the extra removal capacity
  • You want foreshot stripping capability (dephlegmator at high R during foreshots)
  • You prioritize light fruity esters (ethyl acetate, isoamyl acetate) over body/depth
  • You plan to age the spirit (barrel aging adds back body compounds that the column strips)

When mesh-in-pot is better:

  • You want maximum flavor complexity and body in new-make spirit
  • Your wash DMS is moderate (<60 ppb) and 50–70% removal is adequate
  • You make white spirits (unaged) where body compounds matter
  • You prefer a simpler, lower-cost build

6.4 Integration with Accelerated Maturation

The CRO-10 produces new-make spirit optimized for the accelerated aging techniques in barriers.html:

  • Less sulfur to manage during aging (DMS well below threshold from day one)
  • More ester substrate for acid-catalyzed transesterification (§1.1, §1.9)
  • The spirit can proceed directly to oxidative maturation without a copper fining step
  • Higher fusel content is acceptable when maturation will convert fusel alcohols to fruity esters via esterification
