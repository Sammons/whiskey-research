# Supercritical and Pressurized CO2 for Modulating Enzyme Activity in Ethanol-Water Systems

## A Cross-Domain Literature Review for Whiskey Maturation Acceleration

**Date:** 2026-03-22

---

## Executive Summary

This review investigates whether pressurized carbon dioxide (50-300 bar) can be used to modulate enzyme activity in ethanol-water systems relevant to whiskey maturation acceleration. Five specific cross-domain hypotheses are examined: (1) the effect of CO2 pressure on alcohol dehydrogenase activity, (2) whether CO2-expanded liquids can reduce effective ethanol concentration to mitigate substrate inhibition, (3) published examples of biocatalysis with thermostable enzymes in scCO2, (4) the potential for carbonic acid formation to buffer Fischer esterification, and (5) the combination of CO2 pressurization with acoustic cavitation. The literature reveals that while enzymes -- including ADH -- are demonstrably active in supercritical CO2, the direct application to a 40% ABV spirit system faces fundamental phase-behavior and enzyme stability challenges. However, three promising strategies emerge: (a) CO2-expanded liquid media for tuning solvent properties and water activity, (b) lipase-catalyzed flavor ester synthesis in scCO2 as a separate processing step, and (c) a novel combination of pressurized CO2 with ultrasound exploiting the "soft cavitation" phenomenon.

## 1. ADH Activity Under CO2 Pressure

### 1.1 Landmark Demonstration: ADH Is Active in Supercritical CO2

**Matsuda, T., Harada, T. & Nakamura, K. (2000).** "Alcohol dehydrogenase is active in supercritical carbon dioxide." *Chemical Communications*, 2000, 1367-1368. DOI: 10.1039/B004069J

- First demonstration that alcohol dehydrogenase retains catalytic activity in scCO2
- Used ADH from *Geotrichum candidum* at **35 C and 10 MPa (100 bar)**
- Catalyzed asymmetric reduction of o-fluoroacetophenone to (S)-1-(o-fluorophenyl)ethanol
- **Yield: 81% after 12 hours** with >99% enantiomeric excess
- 2-Propanol served as the hydrogen donor / reductant
- All substrates reduced by ADH in scCO2 showed very high enantioselectivity (>99% ee)
- **Status:** Published, experimentally validated
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** This is a proof-of-principle that ADH enzymes survive and function at 100 bar CO2. However, the reaction was conducted with substrates dissolved in scCO2 with minimal water, not in an aqueous ethanol mixture. The key question -- whether pressurized CO2 could modulate ADH kinetics in a high-ethanol aqueous medium -- was not addressed.

### 1.2 Semi-Continuous Flow ADH Process in scCO2

**Matsuda, T., Harada, T. & Nakamura, K. (2003).** "Biocatalytic reduction of ketones by a semi-continuous flow process using supercritical carbon dioxide." *Chemical Communications*, 2003, 1578-1579. DOI: 10.1039/B304412E

- Extended the 2000 work to a continuous-flow system using immobilized whole cells of *G. candidum* as catalyst
- Operated at 35 C, 10 MPa in scCO2 flowing through a packed column
- Achieved **>99% ee** and **higher space-time yield** than the batch process
- Demonstrated that immobilized cells protect the ADH from CO2-induced deactivation
- **Status:** Published, experimentally validated
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** The continuous-flow packed-bed design is directly relevant to a home reactor concept. However, the system again uses scCO2 as the sole solvent, not ethanol-water.

### 1.3 Review: Organic Synthesis Using Enzymes in scCO2

**Matsuda, T., Harada, T. & Nakamura, K. (2004).** "Organic synthesis using enzymes in supercritical carbon dioxide." *Green Chemistry*, 6, 440-444. DOI: 10.1039/B404564P

- Review covering enzymatic asymmetric reduction (ADH), kinetic resolution (lipase), and carboxylation (decarboxylase) in scCO2
- Key principle: scCO2 at 10 MPa has a log P value of approximately 1.0, placing it between hydrophilic and hydrophobic solvents
- Despite the lowest log P among solvents tested, **the reaction rate in CO2 was the highest** in the pressure range over 10 MPa
- Reaction rate is strongly pressure-dependent in CO2 -- this is a tunable parameter
- **Status:** Published review
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** The pressure-tunability of reaction rate is potentially exploitable. If ADH activity in ethanol-water could be modulated by CO2 pressure (even modestly), it would represent a new control parameter for the maturation reactor.

### 1.4 Mechanisms of Enzyme Deactivation in scCO2

**Wimmer, Z. & Zarevucka, M. (2010).** "A Review on the Effects of Supercritical Carbon Dioxide on Enzyme Activity." *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*, 11(1), 233-253. DOI: 10.3390/ijms11010233

- Comprehensive review of enzyme behavior under scCO2 conditions
- **Four deactivation mechanisms identified:**
  1. **Water stripping:** scCO2 removes structurally essential water from enzyme surfaces, disrupting the hydration shell required for activity. Adding small amounts of water (0.5-5% v/v) to substrates reverses this deactivation.
  2. **CO2 carbamylation:** CO2 reacts reversibly with free amino groups (N-terminus, lysine epsilon-amino) to form carbamate adducts (-NH-COO-). This can alter active site geometry.
  3. **pH depression:** Dissolved CO2 forms carbonic acid, locally reducing pH near enzyme active sites, potentially shifting protonation states of catalytic residues.
  4. **Conformational disruption:** At very high pressures (>150 MPa), bulk compression effects alter protein tertiary structure.
- **Key quantitative finding:** At pressures of 100-300 bar and temperatures of 20-90 C, many enzymes retain substantial activity. Pressure-induced deactivation occurs mostly above 150 MPa (1500 bar), far above the 50-100 bar range of interest.
- Dehydrogenases, lipases, phosphatases, oxidases, and amylases are all documented as active in scCO2
- **Status:** Published review of ~50 primary studies
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** The water-stripping mechanism is actually *inverted* in an aqueous ethanol system -- there is excess water present. This means the primary deactivation pathway in neat scCO2 would not apply. However, carbamylation of lysine residues could still be relevant, particularly if it modifies the ADH active site entrance or the NAD+ binding pocket.

### 1.5 CO2 Carbamylation as Post-Translational Modification

**Linthwaite, V.L. et al. (2018).** "The identification of carbon dioxide mediated protein post-translational modifications." *Nature Communications*, 9, 3092. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-018-05475-z

- Systematic identification of CO2 binding sites on proteins via carbamate formation
- CO2 reacts with neutral amine groups (R-NH2) to form anionic carbamates (R-NH-COO-)
- The modification is **reversible** and **site-specific** -- occurs only where amines exist in the unprotonated (NH2) form
- Carbamate formation is favored in environments of **reduced hydration** (consistent with scCO2 stripping water from protein surfaces)
- The negative charge introduced by carbamate may modulate protein-protein interactions and active site architecture
- Classic example: RuBisCO activation requires carbamylation of Lys-201 near the Mg2+ cofactor -- CO2 is both a substrate and an allosteric activator
- **Status:** Published
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** This is a double-edged sword. Carbamylation could inhibit ADH by modifying active-site lysines involved in NAD+ binding. Alternatively, if carbamylation stabilizes a particular conformational state, it could be exploited. For BsADH (the thermostable ADH candidate), the catalytic zinc coordination and the lysine residues involved in substrate binding would need to be mapped against potential carbamylation sites.

### 1.6 Quantitative Assessment: ADH Under CO2 Pressure

Synthesizing findings from sections 1.1-1.5:

| Parameter | Value | Relevance |
|-----------|-------|-----------|
| GcADH activity in scCO2 (10 MPa, 35 C) | 81% yield, 12 hr | ADH functions at 100 bar |
| Pressure range for enzyme activity | 50-300 bar generally tolerated | Within practical reactor range |
| Pressure threshold for deactivation | >150 MPa (1500 bar) | Far above operating range |
| Key deactivation: water stripping | Reversed by adding 0.5-5% water | Not relevant in aqueous system |
| Key deactivation: carbamylation | Reversible, targets free amines | Potentially modulates ADH kinetics |
| Reaction rate pressure-dependence | Strong in scCO2 at >10 MPa | Tunable parameter |

**Bottom line:** ADH is active at 100 bar CO2. In a 40% ABV ethanol-water system pressurized with CO2, the enzyme would face a two-phase or expanded-liquid environment rather than neat scCO2. The effect on ADH activity in this specific medium is **unpublished and speculative** -- it represents a genuinely novel experimental question.

## 2. CO2-Expanded Liquids and Substrate Inhibition

### 2.1 Phase Behavior of CO2-Ethanol-Water Ternary System

**Joung, S.N. et al. (2001).** "High-pressure vapor-liquid equilibria for carbon dioxide + ethanol and carbon dioxide + methanol." *Journal of Chemical & Engineering Data*, 46(1), 79-83. DOI: 10.1021/je00009a012

**Kordikowski, A. et al. (2017).** "Phase Equilibrium Study of the Ternary System CO2 + H2O + Ethanol at Elevated Pressure." *Journal of Supercritical Fluids*, 128, 222-230. DOI: 10.1016/j.supflu.2017.06.001

- The CO2-ethanol binary system has a thermodynamic mixture critical pressure of **7.9 MPa at 313 K (40 C)**
- Below this pressure: no liquid phase volume expansion; CO2 dissolves into the ethanol phase
- Between 7.9 and 8.5 MPa: volume expansion observed -- the liquid phase expands as dissolved CO2 increases
- Above ~8.5 MPa at 40 C: single supercritical phase
- In the ternary CO2-ethanol-water system, the presence of water raises the critical pressure and creates a more complex phase envelope with possible two-phase (liquid-vapor) and three-phase (liquid-liquid-vapor) regions
- **CO2 mole fraction at 50 bar, 40 C in ethanol:** approximately 0.3-0.5 (depending on exact conditions)
- **Status:** Published, well-characterized VLE data
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** At 50 bar (5 MPa) and 40 C, a 40% ethanol-water solution would absorb substantial CO2 without reaching supercritical conditions. The liquid phase would expand moderately. This is the "CO2-expanded liquid" (CXL) regime.

### 2.2 CO2-Expanded Liquids as Tunable Enzyme Reaction Media

**Matsuda, T. (2025).** "Medium engineering of lipase-catalyzed reaction using CO2." *Journal of the American Oil Chemists' Society*. DOI: 10.1002/aocs.12895

- Recent comprehensive review of pressurized CO2 (supercritical, liquid, and CO2-expanded liquid) as media for lipase catalysis
- CO2-expanded liquids (CXLs) operate at **lower pressures** and **wider temperature ranges** than scCO2
- By dissolving CO2 into an organic solvent, the medium's properties are continuously tuned:
  - Dielectric permittivity decreases
  - Solubility parameters decrease
  - Viscosity decreases (improved mass transfer)
  - Surface tension decreases
  - **Water activity decreases** (CO2 displaces water from the liquid phase)
- **Status:** Published review, 2025

**Matsuda, T. et al. (2017).** "CO2-expanded bio-based liquids as novel solvents for enantioselective biocatalysis." *Tetrahedron*, 73(39), 5633-5640. DOI: 10.1016/j.tet.2017.07.048

- CO2-expanded 2-methyltetrahydrofuran (MeTHF) transformed unfavorable solvents into excellent reaction media for CALB-catalyzed transesterification
- Biocatalytic activity was closely correlated to physicochemical properties of the medium, tunable by adjusting CO2 mole fraction
- **Status:** Published

### 2.3 The Substrate Inhibition Hypothesis: Can CXLs Help?

The central problem for ADH in 40% ABV whiskey (see enzyme-cascade-review.md):
- Ethanol concentration = 6,800 mM
- BsADH Km(ethanol) = 0.91 mM
- Substrate saturation ratio = ~7,500x Km
- Dead-end E-NADH-ethanol complex formation dominates kinetics above ~500 mM

**Hypothesis:** Dissolving CO2 into the ethanol-water mixture at 50-100 bar could:
1. **Reduce effective ethanol concentration** by expanding the liquid volume (dilution effect)
2. **Alter the solvation shell around the enzyme**, reducing the local ethanol activity coefficient
3. **Modify ethanol's interaction with the enzyme surface**, potentially disrupting dead-end complex formation

**Quantitative analysis of the dilution effect:**
- At 50 bar and 40 C, CO2 dissolves to approximately 15-25 mol% in 40% ethanol-water
- Volume expansion: approximately 20-40% (conservative estimate from binary ethanol-CO2 data)
- Effective ethanol concentration after expansion: ~4,900-5,700 mM (from 6,800 mM)
- This is a **modest reduction** (16-28%) and still far above the substrate inhibition threshold of ~500 mM
- **Conclusion:** Volume expansion alone is insufficient to solve the substrate inhibition problem

**However**, the solvation environment change is more nuanced:
- CO2 dissolved in the ethanol-water mixture reduces ethanol's chemical activity coefficient
- The enzyme does not "see" molar concentration per se -- it senses the thermodynamic activity of ethanol in the local microenvironment
- If CO2 preferentially solvates ethanol molecules near the enzyme surface, the effective ethanol activity at the active site could be reduced beyond the simple dilution effect
- This is **speculative** but thermodynamically plausible

**Status:** Novel hypothesis. No published data on ADH activity in CO2-expanded ethanol-water. The CXL biocatalysis literature uses organic solvents (MeTHF, hexane), not aqueous ethanol.

### 2.4 Water Activity Modulation

**Halling, P.J. (1994).** "Thermodynamic predictions for biocatalysis in nonconventional media." *Enzyme and Microbial Technology*, 16, 178-206.

- Water activity (aw) is the key thermodynamic parameter for enzyme catalysis in non-conventional media
- For esterification: low aw (0.3-0.6) favors synthesis; high aw (>0.9) favors hydrolysis
- 40% ABV whiskey: aw ~ 0.95 (strongly hydrolytic conditions)

**CXL effect on water activity:**
- CO2 dissolution reduces aw of the liquid phase by:
  - Diluting water with dissolved CO2
  - Extracting water from the liquid into the CO2-rich vapor phase (at conditions near the phase boundary)
  - Lowering the dielectric permittivity, which changes solvation energetics
- The magnitude of aw reduction is pressure- and composition-dependent
- In a 40% ethanol-water system pressurized with CO2 at 50 bar, aw might decrease from 0.95 to approximately 0.85-0.90 (estimated from binary CXL data)
- This is a **meaningful shift** for esterification equilibrium, though still above the optimal aw of 0.3-0.6 for high ester yields
- **Status:** The principle is published; the quantitative application to ethanol-water CXLs is speculative

## 3. Biocatalysis with Thermostable Enzymes in scCO2

### 3.1 Lipase-Catalyzed Esterification: The Most Developed System

**Romero, M.D. et al. (2005).** "Enzymatic synthesis of isoamyl acetate with immobilized Candida antarctica lipase in supercritical carbon dioxide." *Journal of Supercritical Fluids*, 33(1), 77-84. DOI: 10.1016/j.supflu.2004.04.003

- Synthesized **isoamyl acetate** (banana/pear flavor ester, important in whiskey) from isoamyl alcohol + acetic anhydride
- Catalyzed by immobilized *C. antarctica* lipase B (Novozym 435) in scCO2
- **100% esterification extent** achieved in continuous operation using acetic anhydride as acyl donor
- With acetic acid (instead of anhydride): lower conversion due to water accumulation inhibiting the equilibrium
- Optimal enzyme loading: 6.25 g/mol alcohol
- Also tested Lipozyme RM-IM (*Rhizomucor miehei*): achieved 90% esterification ratio
- Initial reaction rate was **significantly higher in scCO2** than in n-hexane
- **Status:** Published, reproducible
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** Isoamyl acetate is one of the key flavor esters in whiskey. This demonstrates that the exact ester of interest can be synthesized enzymatically in scCO2 with quantitative yield. The catch: the reaction used neat substrates dissolved in scCO2, not a 40% ethanol-water medium.

### 3.2 Butyl Butyrate Kinetics in scCO2

**Habulin, M. & Knez, Z. (2001).** "Kinetics of synthesis of butyl butyrate by esterification and transesterification in supercritical carbon dioxide." *Journal of Chemical Technology and Biotechnology*. DOI: 10.1002/jctb.714

- Enzymatic esterification of butanol + butyric acid to butyl butyrate using Novozym 435 in scCO2
- Maximum esterification rate: **2.22 mol g-1 h-1** at optimal enzyme loading (10 mg)
- Equilibrium conversion: **80% in 1 hour** at 45-50 C
- Kinetics follow Ping-Pong Bi-Bi model with substrate inhibition by both acid and alcohol
- **Status:** Published, quantitative kinetics available
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** Butyl butyrate (fruity ester) is another whiskey-relevant flavor compound. The high reaction rate and conversion in scCO2 confirm the viability of enzymatic ester synthesis under pressure.

### 3.3 Lipase Stability Under scCO2 Exposure

**Habulin, M. & Knez, Z. (2001).** "Activity and stability of lipases from different sources in supercritical carbon dioxide and near-critical propane." *Journal of Chemical Technology and Biotechnology*, 76, 1260-1266. DOI: 10.1002/jctb.714

- Tested lipases from *Pseudomonas fluorescens*, *Rhizopus javanicus*, *R. niveus*, and porcine pancreas
- Stability assessed at **100 bar and 300 bar**, 40 C, in scCO2 and near-critical propane
- **Key findings:**
  - Lipases generally retained activity after exposure to scCO2, but with significant variation by source
  - *P. fluorescens* lipase showed best stability in scCO2
  - Near-critical propane was gentler on enzymes than scCO2 (no carbamylation mechanism)
  - scCO2-specific deactivation attributed to: (a) water stripping, (b) carbamylation, (c) local pH depression
  - Adding 5% water to the system partially mitigated deactivation
- **Status:** Published
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** Confirms that lipases tolerate the 50-100 bar range but need water supplementation for stability -- which would be inherently provided in a 40% ABV system.

### 3.4 Lipase at Extreme Temperatures in scCO2/Ionic Liquid Biphasic Systems

**Lozano, P. et al. (2003).** "Lipase catalysis in ionic liquids and supercritical carbon dioxide at 150 degrees C." *Biotechnology Progress*, 19(2), 380-382. DOI: 10.1021/bp025759o

- Free and immobilized *C. antarctica* lipase B (CALB) dissolved in ionic liquids were used as catalyst in scCO2
- Operated at **120 C and 150 C** at 10 MPa
- **Excellent activity, stability, and enantioselectivity** recorded in continuous operation
- Ionic liquid ([BMIM][PF6]) acted as the enzyme microenvironment; scCO2 acted as extractive phase
- The IL-stabilized CALB retained activity for extended periods at temperatures where free enzyme would rapidly denature
- **Status:** Published
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** Demonstrates that enzyme stability at high temperatures in scCO2 is achievable with appropriate co-solvents. An IL-stabilized lipase in a scCO2/ethanol-water biphasic system is an intriguing design concept.

### 3.5 Membrane Reactor with CALB in scCO2

**Lozano, P. et al. (2004).** "Membrane reactor with immobilized Candida antarctica lipase B for ester synthesis in supercritical carbon dioxide." *Journal of Supercritical Fluids*, 29(1-2), 121-128. DOI: 10.1016/S0896-8446(03)00050-0

- CALB immobilized on a membrane in a continuous-flow scCO2 reactor
- Achieved continuous ester synthesis with integrated product separation
- scCO2 as mobile phase extracts products as they form, driving equilibrium toward synthesis
- **Status:** Published
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** The membrane reactor concept could be adapted to separate the enzyme environment (scCO2-rich, low water) from the spirit (40% ethanol-water). Substrates (acetic acid, higher alcohols) would partition between phases, with ester products extracted into the scCO2 phase and collected upon depressurization.

### 3.6 Terpinyl Acetate Synthesis

**Lee, W.G. et al. (2010).** "Synthesis of terpinyl acetate by lipase-catalyzed esterification in supercritical carbon dioxide." *Bioresource Technology*, 101(7), 2687-2690. DOI: 10.1016/j.biortech.2009.11.077

- Terpinyl acetate from alpha-terpineol + acetic anhydride in scCO2
- Five different lipases compared; Lipozyme RM IM (*R. miehei*) was the most thermostable and robust
- Lipozyme RM IM is described as "a very thermostable and robust catalyst" that retains activity across many organic solvents and scCO2
- **Status:** Published
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** Terpene esters (linalool acetate, terpinyl acetate) contribute to whiskey complexity. Their enzymatic synthesis in scCO2 is demonstrated. This would work as a separate processing step, not in-situ in the spirit.

### 3.7 Summary: Thermostable Enzyme Biocatalysis in scCO2

| Enzyme | Reaction | Conditions | Conversion | Reference |
|--------|----------|------------|------------|-----------|
| CALB (N435) | Isoamyl acetate synthesis | scCO2, 10 MPa, continuous | 100% | Romero 2005 |
| CALB (N435) | Butyl butyrate synthesis | scCO2, 45-50 C | 80% in 1 hr | Habulin & Knez 2001 |
| CALB in IL | Transesterification | scCO2/[BMIM][PF6], 150 C | High | Lozano 2003 |
| CALB on membrane | Ester synthesis | scCO2, continuous flow | Continuous | Lozano 2004 |

**Bottom line:** scCO2 biocatalysis with thermostable lipases is mature (TRL 5-6 for esterification). Flavor ester synthesis in scCO2 achieves near-quantitative yields. The key whiskey-relevant esters (isoamyl acetate, ethyl butyrate, terpinyl acetate) have all been synthesized enzymatically in scCO2.

## 4. Carbonic Acid Formation and pH Buffering for Esterification

### 4.1 CO2-Water Carbonate Chemistry Under Pressure

When CO2 dissolves in water, it establishes the following equilibrium chain:

```
CO2(g) <-> CO2(aq)                    (Henry's Law: KH)
CO2(aq) + H2O <-> H2CO3               (hydration: Kh ~ 1.7 x 10^-3)
H2CO3 <-> H+ + HCO3-                  (Ka1 = 4.3 x 10^-7, pKa1 = 6.35)
HCO3- <-> H+ + CO3^2-                 (Ka2 = 4.8 x 10^-11, pKa2 = 10.33)
```

**Key quantitative points under pressure:**
- Henry's Law constant for CO2 in water at 25 C: KH ~ 3.4 x 10^-2 mol/L/atm
- At 50 bar (~49 atm): dissolved CO2 ~ 1.7 M (assuming ideal Henry's Law; actual is somewhat lower due to non-ideality)
- At 100 bar: dissolved CO2 approaches saturation; at this pressure and 40 C, the system may be near or in the two-phase CXL regime
- Only ~0.26% of dissolved CO2 forms H2CO3 (Kh ~ 2.6 x 10^-3)
- At 50 bar: [H2CO3] ~ 4.4 mM; [H+] from Ka1 ~ 1.4 x 10^-3 M; predicted pH ~ 2.85
- **The bicarbonate/carbonic acid buffer system operates near pH 6.35** (at the pKa1), but the dissolved CO2 at high pressure drives the equilibrium strongly acidic

### 4.2 pH Effect on Fischer Esterification

Fischer esterification is acid-catalyzed:
```
R-COOH + R'-OH <-> R-COO-R' + H2O    (catalyzed by H+)
```

- Mechanism requires protonation of the carboxylic acid carbonyl oxygen
- Optimal conditions: pH < 3, with strong mineral acid (H2SO4, HCl) as catalyst
- Equilibrium constant Keq is small; driving requires excess alcohol or water removal
- In whiskey at 40% ethanol, the ethanol is in massive excess (6.8 M), which already drives the equilibrium toward ester formation
- The limiting factor is: (a) acid catalyst strength and (b) water removal

**Can carbonic acid serve as the acid catalyst?**
- H2CO3 is a weak acid (pKa 6.35 as "apparent" including CO2(aq); true pKa of molecular H2CO3 ~ 3.6)
- At 50 bar CO2, the solution pH reaches ~2.8-3.0 -- this is in the **marginal range** for Fischer esterification catalysis
- Compared to H2SO4 (pKa ~ -3): carbonic acid is approximately 6 orders of magnitude weaker
- Fischer esterification rates scale with [H+]: at pH 3 vs. pH 1 (typical H2SO4 catalysis), the rate would be ~100x slower
- **However:** The rate penalty is partly offset by the massive ethanol excess and elevated temperature (50-70 C) in the maturation reactor

### 4.3 pH Buffering Advantage

**The real advantage is not as a catalyst, but as a buffer:**
- In the maturation reactor, multiple acid-producing processes run simultaneously (oxidation of ethanol to acetic acid, oak tannin extraction)
- Uncontrolled acidification degrades spirit quality (vinegar notes)
- The CO2/HCO3- buffer system at 50 bar would buffer the solution at approximately pH 3.0-3.5
- This is:
  - Acidic enough to promote slow Fischer esterification
  - Not so acidic as to damage oak compounds or produce harsh flavors
  - Self-regulating: if pH drops below ~3, the equilibrium shifts to consume H+ (forming CO2(aq) -> CO2(g)); if pH rises above ~3.5, more CO2 dissolves and dissociates
- The buffer capacity is substantial at 50 bar: approximately 1-2 molar equivalents of buffering in the CO2/H2CO3/HCO3- system

**Toews, K.L. et al. (1995).** "pH-Defining Equilibrium between Water and Supercritical CO2. Influence on SFE of Organics and Metal Chelates." *Analytical Chemistry*, 67(22), 4040-4043. DOI: 10.1021/ac00118a002

- Measured pH of water equilibrated with scCO2 at various pressures
- At 70 bar and 25 C: pH = **2.80** (measured, not calculated)
- At 100 bar and 25 C: pH = **2.83** (slight increase due to non-ideal behavior)
- At higher temperatures (40 C): pH increases slightly (~3.0-3.1) due to reduced CO2 solubility
- **Status:** Published, experimentally validated
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** The measured pH of ~2.8-3.1 under 70-100 bar CO2 confirms the buffering analysis. In an ethanol-water system, CO2 solubility is higher than in pure water (ethanol enhances CO2 dissolution), so the pH could be somewhat lower. This is directly applicable as a gentle acid catalyst and pH buffer for the maturation reactor.

### 4.4 Quantitative Feasibility Assessment

| Parameter | Value | Assessment |
|-----------|-------|------------|
| pH at 50 bar CO2 in water | ~2.8-3.0 | Marginal for Fischer esterification |
| pH at 50 bar CO2 in 40% EtOH-H2O | ~2.5-2.8 (estimated) | Improved due to higher CO2 solubility |
| Fischer esterification rate vs. H2SO4 catalysis | ~100x slower | Partially compensated by time/temperature |
| Buffer capacity at 50 bar | ~1-2 M equivalents | Excellent for preventing over-acidification |
| Reversibility | Complete upon depressurization | Clean removal of catalyst -- no residual acid |

**Status:** The pH measurements under CO2 pressure are published. The application to Fischer esterification in spirits is **novel and speculative** -- no published data exist for this specific use. The buffering application is a genuinely useful insight: CO2 pressurization provides a removable, self-buffering acid catalyst with zero residue.

## 5. CO2 Pressurization Combined with Acoustic Cavitation

### 5.1 Soft Cavitation Under CO2: The Compton Discovery

**Marken, F., Akkermans, R.P. & Compton, R.G. (1998).** "High-Pressure Sonoelectrochemistry in Aqueous Solution: Soft Cavitation under CO2." *Journal of Physical Chemistry A*, 102(45), 8888-8893. DOI: 10.1021/jp9815964

- Developed a novel high-pressure sonoelectrochemical cell operating up to 60 bar
- Studied electrochemical reactions at a Pt microdisk electrode under ultrasound + pressurized gas
- **Critical discovery:** With CO2 as the pressurizing gas, the **cavitation threshold decreased** at higher pressures (>40 bar)
- With argon, cavitation threshold increased monotonically with pressure (as expected from Blake threshold theory)
- With CO2 at >40 bar, the threshold **decreased** -- meaning cavitation becomes *easier* to induce at higher pressure
- This "soft cavitation" under CO2 is attributed to:
  1. The high vapor pressure of CO2 counteracting hydrostatic pressure
  2. Possible formation of a liquid CO2 micro-phase within collapsing bubbles
  3. Dissolution of CO2 into growing bubbles, lowering the surface tension
- Cavitation under CO2 is "softer" than under argon -- lower collapse temperatures and pressures, but still sufficient for sonochemical effects
- **Status:** Published, experimentally validated
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** This is an extraordinarily promising finding. It means that at 50+ bar CO2, you can still generate acoustic cavitation with standard ultrasonic equipment (20 kHz), despite the elevated pressure. The cavitation would be "softer" (lower peak temperatures) but could still drive:
  - Enhanced mass transfer (extraction from oak)
  - Radical generation (though reduced vs. argon)
  - Emulsification and mixing effects

### 5.2 Cavitation-Induced Reactions in High-Pressure CO2

**Kuijpers, M.W.A., van Eck, D., Kemmere, M.F. & Keurentjes, J.T.F. (2002).** "Cavitation-Induced Reactions in High-Pressure Carbon Dioxide." *Science*, 298(5600), 1969-1971. DOI: 10.1126/science.1078022

- Demonstrated that **acoustic cavitation occurs in liquid CO2** (not aqueous solution)
- The high vapor pressure of CO2 facilitates cavitation at relatively low acoustic intensities
- Used cavitation-induced radical formation for polymerization of methyl methacrylate in liquid CO2
- Produced **high-molecular-weight polymers** -- confirming that sonochemically generated radicals are reactive in the CO2 medium
- The required threshold pressure for cavitation could be exceeded at a relatively low acoustic intensity
- **Status:** Published in *Science* (high-profile)
- **Relevance:** Demonstrates that sonochemistry is feasible in pressurized CO2 environments. However, there is ongoing debate -- a 2025 publication (below) challenges whether true acoustic cavitation occurs in high-pressure liquid CO2.

### 5.3 Challenge: Does Cavitation Actually Occur in High-Pressure CO2 Liquid?

**Li, C. et al. (2025).** "Ultrasonic cavitation did not occur in high-pressure CO2 liquid." *Nonlinear Engineering*. DOI: 10.1515/nleng-2025-0112

- Challenges the Kuijpers (2002) conclusions through theoretical analysis
- Argues that the vapor pressure within bubbles in high-pressure liquid CO2 cannot remain constant or be balanced by static pressure, undermining the conditions required for acoustic cavitation
- Proposes that the observed effects (radical generation, polymerization) may be due to **acoustic streaming** and **thermal effects** rather than true cavitation
- **Status:** Published, but contested -- this is an active scientific debate

### 5.4 Ultrasound-Assisted Supercritical CO2 Extraction (UAE-SFE)

**Santos, P. et al. (2021).** "Extraction of natural products using supercritical fluids and pressurized liquids assisted by ultrasound: Current status and trends." *Ultrasonics Sonochemistry*, 74, 105584. DOI: 10.1016/j.ultsonch.2021.105584

- Comprehensive review of combined ultrasound + scCO2 processes
- Key insight: the mechanisms in UAE-SFE **differ from traditional ultrasonic extraction**
  - Bubble cavitation rarely occurs due to pressurization and lack of phase boundary
  - Instead: acoustic streaming, vibration, and **enhanced mass transfer** are the dominant effects
  - Mass transfer convective coefficients increase due to turbulence
  - Cell wall disruption (for plant material) occurs through repeated acoustic stress
- **For extraction of phenolics from plant matrices** (directly relevant to oak chip extraction):
  - Combined ultrasound + scCO2 showed extraction rates up to **3-5x higher** than scCO2 alone
  - The ultrasound enhances accessibility of extraction sites by disrupting cellular structure
- **Status:** Published review
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** The enhanced extraction of phenolics from oak under combined US + pressurized CO2 is directly applicable. Even without true cavitation, acoustic streaming at 50-100 bar CO2 would significantly accelerate extraction of oak lactones, vanillin, tannins, and other wood compounds into the spirit.

### 5.5 Combined US + High-Pressure CO2 for Extraction Enhancement

**Riera, E. et al. (2004).** "Mechanisms and effects of ultrasound-assisted supercritical CO2 extraction." *Trends in Food Science & Technology*, 19(3), 174-183.

**Balachandran, S. et al. (2006).** "Ultrasonic enhancement of the supercritical extraction from ginger." *Ultrasonics Sonochemistry*, 13(6), 471-479. DOI: 10.1016/j.ultsonch.2005.11.006

- At 250 bar scCO2 with 20 kHz ultrasound: extraction rate of ginger oleoresin increased by **30%**
- The ultrasonic enhancement was attributed to improved external mass transfer (not cavitation)
- **Status:** Published
- **Feasibility for 40% ABV system:** This confirms that even at pressures well above the cavitation threshold, ultrasound provides meaningful extraction enhancement through acoustic streaming.

### 5.6 Sono-Fenton and Sonoelectrochemistry Under Pressure

**Oturan, M.A. & Sires, I. (2009).** "Sonoelectro-Fenton process: A novel hybrid technique." *Journal of Electroanalytical Chemistry*, 624(1-2), 329-332. DOI: 10.1016/j.jelechem.2008.08.005

- Combined ultrasound with electrochemical H2O2 generation (electro-Fenton) at ambient pressure
- Synergistic effect: ultrasound enhances mass transfer to electrode + disperses Fenton reagent
- Demonstrated for pollutant degradation; the chemistry (OH radical generation from H2O2/Fe2+) is the same mechanism involved in whiskey maturation oxidation

**Potential extension to pressurized CO2 (speculative):**
- Combining the Compton "soft cavitation under CO2" with the electro-Fenton mechanism would create:
  - Electrochemical H2O2 generation at the cathode
  - Fenton radical production (Fe2+ + H2O2 -> Fe3+ + OH* + OH-)
  - Acoustic dispersion and mass transfer enhancement
  - CO2-buffered pH at ~3 (optimal for Fenton chemistry, which requires pH 2.8-3.5)
  - Simultaneous NADH -> NAD+ anodic oxidation (for enzyme cofactor recycling)
- **Status:** This specific combination is **entirely novel** -- unpublished. Individual components are published.

### 6.5 Recommended Strategy: Two-Stage Process

**Stage 1: CO2-Pressurized Oak Extraction + Mild Esterification (50 bar, 50-65 C)**
- Spirit + oak chips in pressurized vessel with 20 kHz ultrasonic probe
- CO2 at 50 bar provides: enhanced extraction (acoustic streaming), pH buffering (~3), mild acid catalysis
- Duration: 6-24 hours (equivalent to months of barrel extraction)
- No enzymes required; purely physical/chemical acceleration
- **Confidence: High** (all components published)

**Stage 2: Enzymatic Ester Polishing in scCO2 (100 bar, 50 C)**
- Depressurize, add acetic acid + higher alcohols (from Stage 1 oak extraction)
- Repressurize with scCO2 containing immobilized lipase (CALB or Lipozyme RM IM)
- Operate in the low-water-activity regime inherent to scCO2
- Synthesize flavor esters: isoamyl acetate, ethyl butyrate, ethyl hexanoate
- Duration: 1-4 hours (based on published kinetics)
- **Confidence: Medium-High** (published for pure substrates; in spirit matrix untested)
